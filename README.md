# Pet Friendly Hotels: How to Spot the Fakes, Price the Pet Fee Before You Book, and Cut the Nightly Rate with Cashback

Searching "pet friendly hotels" is easy. Getting a straight answer out of the results is not. You filter for pets, pick a nice-looking property, book a non-refundable rate, and then find out at check-in that the "pet friendly" label meant dogs under 20 pounds, or that there's a $150 cleaning fee nobody mentioned until the card was already charged.

The frustrating part is that most of this is knowable before you pay. Pet policies are published, fees follow a handful of predictable structures, and the booking channel you use decides whether you also get a few percent back on a spend that's usually your biggest trip cost. This guide walks through the checking, the math, and the cashback layer, using ShopBack as the concrete example since its travel partners cover the platforms most pet-friendly inventory actually lives on.

## What "pet friendly" means on a booking site (spoiler: not much)

A pet-friendly badge tells you the property accepts *some* pets under *some* conditions. It does not tell you the fee, the weight cap, the breed restrictions, or whether the policy covers cats.

The numbers are messier than most travellers expect. Roch Dog, which grades hotel pet friendliness, collected data on more than 2,000 pet-friendly hotels and found that only 12% of them allowed cats — which is why cat owners tend to treat the phrase "pet friendly" with suspicion. Weight limits are the other trap: Hilton's pet-friendly properties typically accept dogs up to 75 pounds, but individual hotels inside the same brand can set their own caps, and properties that quietly allow only small dogs exist in every chain.

Pet fees, meanwhile, are not small change. Booking.com's own guidance puts typical pet fees in the range of £10–50 per night, and AARP's reporting on the subject notes that fees can add hundreds to thousands of dollars a year for frequent pet travellers. That variance is the whole game. Two hotels with the same nightly rate and the same "pet friendly" badge can be $75 apart on a five-night stay once the fee structure is applied.

So the first rule of searching for pet friendly hotels: treat the badge as a starting filter, not an answer.

## The three checks that save you from a bad check-in

**1. Use the booking sites' pet-specific filters, then ignore the label.**

Booking.com surfaces a "pets stay free" badge on properties that don't charge at all, which is the single most useful filter if your goal is avoiding fees rather than just being allowed in. It's a real distinction: free, versus allowed-but-charged. Most full-service search pages also let you filter by pet policy, so you can cut the list down before you start reading fine print. Agoda and Trip.com both carry pet-policy fields on listings, though the depth varies by region and the data comes from the property, not from a regulator.

**2. Call the property. Yes, actually call.**

Hotel websites go stale. The AARP piece on cutting pet fees is blunt about this: published pet fees can change week to week, and the recommendation from people who run pet-travel resources is to phone the hotel and ask exactly what you'll pay, including any separate refundable damage deposit. A web listing that says "$25 pet fee" may not mention the $100 deposit that gets held for a week.

The call is also where you find out about size limits before you commit to a non-refundable rate. If the hotel's site says "pet friendly" without specifying a weight cap and you're travelling with a 90-pound dog, a five-minute call protects the whole booking cost.

**3. Check whether your animal is covered at all.**

Cats, birds, rabbits and reptiles are frequently excluded even where dogs are welcome. Service animals are a different category entirely: under the ADA in the US, hotels cannot charge a pet fee for a service dog, and no pet fee or deposit applies. That exemption does not extend to emotional support animals in the same way, and hotels are allowed to ask whether the dog is required because of a disability and what task it performs.

## The pet fee math that never shows up in the search results

Fees come in two shapes, and which one is cheaper depends entirely on how long you're staying. A per-night fee with a cap rewards short stays. A flat per-stay fee rewards long ones… usually, but sometimes the flat fee steps up once you cross a threshold.

Real published structures, to make this concrete:

| Property (published policy) | Fee structure | What one dog costs on a 5-night stay |
| --- | --- | --- |
| La Quinta by Wyndham (most locations) | Optional $25 per night, capped at $75 per room per stay | $75 |
| Hilton Garden Inn Hattiesburg | $75 for 1–4 nights, $125 for 5+ nights | $125 |
| Hotel 116, a Coast Hotel (Bellevue, WA) | $25 per pet, per stay, max 2 pets | $25 |

Note that last one. Some independent and boutique properties use flat per-stay fees that barely move the needle, which is why the cheapest hotel at the top of the search results isn't automatically the cheapest once a dog is in the room. In the table above, the "expensive" boutique option beats both chain properties on fee alone.

Two more structural details worth knowing before you book:

- **Weight-based pricing exists.** Some properties charge less for pets under roughly 24 pounds, and some waive fees for cats while charging for dogs. If you're travelling with a small animal, it's worth checking whether a lower tier exists.
- **Multi-pet bookings get expensive fast.** When the fee is per pet rather than per room, a two-dog trip can double the charge. A few properties cap the total or offer a multi-pet package, but you usually have to ask.

Also worth a mention: some brands periodically run promos that waive the fee entirely. Staypineapple, a boutique chain with a standard $29.95-per-night pet fee, has run a "Pineapple Pup Plus One" offer that eliminated the fee for bookings made during its promo window using a code, for stays through the end of the following year. If you know your dates months ahead, a brand-level waiver is worth more than any cashback percentage. Check whether the offer is currently live rather than assuming it is.

1. Note the price and the pet policy before activating anything.

## A booking flow that works for pet travel

Step by step, this is the sequence that avoids both bad check-ins and pointless rebates:

1. **Fix your non-negotiables first.** Number of pets, weight, species. This determines whether you're searching for "pets allowed" or the much narrower "pets stay free".
2. **Search two or three platforms, not one.** Agoda, Booking.com and Trip.com draw from overlapping but not identical inventory, and small independent pet-friendly properties are exactly the kind that appear on one platform and not the others.
3. **Filter, then read the policy text.** "Pets stay free" is the strongest filter. After that, open the property's policy section rather than trusting the badge.
4. **Call the shortlisted properties.** Confirm fee, deposit, weight cap, and whether your animal type is covered.
5. **Compare net cost, not headline rate.** Nightly rate + (fee × nights, or flat fee) minus any cashback. Include parking if the pet gear means you're driving.
6. **Book through the cashback route only if the post-click price holds.** Otherwise book direct and skip the rebate.
7. **Start the booking from ShopBack, not from the OTA app.** Cashback tracks on the referral click, and starting inside the OTA directly means no tracking at all.

## Questions that come up when you travel with a pet

**Do I have to pay a pet fee for a service dog?**
No. In the US, hotels cannot charge pet fees or deposits for service dogs under the ADA, provided the dog is well-behaved and under the handler's control. Emotional support animals fall under different rules and hotel policies vary more widely.

**Are cats covered by "pet friendly"?**
Often not. The Roch Dog dataset found only about 12% of pet-friendly hotels accepted cats, so if you're travelling with a cat, filter for it explicitly and confirm by phone.

**What happens to my cashback if I cancel the hotel?**
It reverses. Cashback is a share of a real, completed sale, so a cancelled or refunded booking means no commission to share and the pending amount drops to zero. Cancellation fees and partial refunds are handled by the hotel per its own policy.

**Does using a cashback link hurt my credit card points?**
No. Cashback is paid by the merchant's affiliate commission, not by the card network, so your card still earns whatever rewards it normally would on the transaction. Miles and cashback stack.
